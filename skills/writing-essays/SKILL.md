# Writing essays

An essay exists to change how a reader thinks about something. Most weak essays fail before the sentences start: the idea is vague, too small for the material, or larger than the evidence. Work on the thinking first, then the shape, and the prose last.

## 1. Find the idea

Read all the material: the draft, notes, data, examples and any context the writer gives. Then write down, for yourself:

- **the idea**: one or two sentences the reader should believe or understand at the end that they did not at the start
- **the reader**: who they are and what they currently believe, assume or do
- **the stakes**: what goes differently for the reader once they hold the idea

Look for the most general idea the material earns. A case study usually shows something more general than its own case: a property of a class of systems, a recurring tradeoff, a wrong default most people share. Name that generalization, then test it against the material. Every main section should support it, and it should survive the most obvious counterexample. If it only holds under conditions, those conditions are part of the idea.

When the material supports no idea yet, say so and name what is missing, such as a result, a comparison or the writer's own judgement. Ask the writer rather than inventing it.

Completion criterion: you can state the idea, the reader's starting point and the stakes, and point to the material that carries each.

## 2. Build the argument

Order the piece by what the reader needs next to accept the idea, starting from where they already are. The shape follows the material. A mechanism suggests problem, cause and consequence; competing designs suggest a comparison on one axis; a surprising result suggests a puzzle and its explanation; a skill suggests a worked example. Chronology is one option among these, useful when sequence itself is the evidence.

Move up and down the ladder of abstraction. Each general claim needs a concrete instance close by: a number, a row of data, a line of code, an event. Each instance needs its meaning stated, so the reader knows what it shows. A piece that stays low reads as a report; one that stays high reads as opinion.

Anticipate the reader's strongest doubt and deal with it where it would arise: answer it, or concede what it gets right. State where the idea stops applying.

Open with what makes this reader want the answer: the question, the cost of getting it wrong or the claim itself. End on what the idea changes, whether a consequence, an open question or the next problem it exposes.

Completion criterion: every section earns its place by moving the reader toward the idea, and you could say in one line what each one contributes.

## 3. Write in the writer's voice

The voice belongs to the writer. Take it from their draft or notes: their register, their language, how formally they speak, how they address the reader and where they use humour. Make the commitment of each sentence match its evidence: firm where the writer knows, explicit about guesses, opinions and open questions.

Write sentences a reader understands on the first pass. State each insight once, in plain words, at the point the evidence supports it, and let the material carry its force.

## Editing someone else's essay

The argument, the voice and the attribution stay the author's. Strengthen the idea, the order and the transitions, and cut what does not serve the idea. Keep their technical examples, data, code and links intact unless an example is wrong.

Keep the author's claims in the text as the author's. When you doubt a fact, find a contradiction, or think a claim exceeds its evidence, list it in a separate note for the author rather than silently rewriting around it. Fix only plain errors inside the text, such as arithmetic that the essay's own data contradicts, and mention each fix in the note.

Every fact, experience, quotation and result in the output comes from the source. Mark any illustration you add as hypothetical.

## Return

Return the essay first. Then give a short note covering the idea as you understood it, the structural changes and why, and any doubts or corrections for the author. Before returning, compare the result with the source and check every claim whose meaning, certainty or attribution changed.
